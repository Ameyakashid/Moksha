# MOKSHA

This is everything Ameya has said about this game, compiled from his own messages across several design conversations (Jul 24 – Aug 18, 2026). It contains only his ideas, with nothing added or interpreted. Where an idea changed over time, every version is kept in the order he said it, and nothing has been resolved for him.

---

## Name

**Moksha.**

---

## Genre and feel

- A roguelite with fast-paced, smooth gameplay of the Dead Cells type. It is side-on, "on the side like those games."
- There are 4 floors.
- Scaling should feel like Risk of Rain 2: the player gets OP things and has fun with them.
- The objects are real. When an attack hits them, they go swinging.
- He wants a nostalgia-type feel where the people and objects have physics.
- Combat should be Dead Cells-fast and smooth, but every hit lands with a **nanopause**, and the sound effects are **buttery dopamine hits**.
- Animation is anime-style, built on **micropauses** that convey speed and weight. They are used in cinematic moments, and the gameplay follows the same approach.

---

## The core mechanic: giving up senses

- On every floor you have to give up a sense.
- Losing a sense is balanced by gaining something just as OP. (He hadn't yet worked out what each of those gains is.)
- The mechanic is an inversion: per floor, you choose where you take the hit.
- The senses intermingle. When you lose sight, you rely on sound, smell, and maybe agility.
- **Swiss army knife → scalpel.** Having every sense is good, like a Swiss army knife, but the interactions between them cause clutter. As you cut senses away you are forced to hyper-specialize, like a scalpel. There is less visual clutter and less of every other kind. You are losing tools but specializing in one to make up for what's missing.
  - Example: if you're blind and see both a smell aura and sound outlines on people, that sounds annoying. Cutting smell then means less visual clutter.
- When you lose one sense, the others sharpen, and by a larger degree.
- The senses should be a bit overwhelming at the start, so that specializing feels good.
- **Explanation in the fiction (later idea):** when you lose a sense in one powered-up version of your body, you gain it in another. That is the explanation for losing and getting things.

### How many you cut
- Ideally the player loses about 2 senses to be decently buffed for the boss.
- They can remove up to 3, because there are 4 floors.
- The player also has the option of taking no debuffs at all. In that case, the counter-buff for each debuff they *do* take has to be really strong, either damage-wise or gameplay-wise.

### What the sense list was, as it evolved
1. **First pitch:** sight, hearing, smell, and "maybe a leg or two."
2. **Later:** vision, hearing, agi/str, and "smell or something." The last slot was still to be workshopped.
3. **Later still:** "Str and agi is one of the choices, and the others are the 3 senses."
4. **Then:** intellect, as a companion sprite, was added as another sense (see Intellect below).

## Each sense

### Sight
- When sight is lost, it is replaced by **pulses moving out from the protagonist**. These show the player using their other senses to see.
- Objects that break, or that you throw, make noise. With a ton of noise you can almost see in real time, because the waves overlap.
- From the start (with the str or agi lens), you have the sonar but don't need it until sight goes. When sight goes, the sonar becomes slightly better and properly useful.

### Hearing
- **First pitch:** while you have hearing, parry animations flash yellow. When you lose it, they don't anymore.
- **Revised:** losing hearing is instead replaced by enemies briefly slowing down before they finish their attack, which gives you a chance to parry or dodge.
- If hearing goes, the pulses go with it. Sight becomes sharper instead:
  - More vivid, sharp colours.
  - Buffs to visual acuity, such as seeing the perfect parry timing because it flashes a quick colour.
  - The more senses you lose while keeping only sight, the better sight gets.
- "And likewise for all senses": whichever sense you keep last gets better the more you cut.

### Smell
- **First pitch:** while you have smell, you can see where items and secrets are, and see into the rooms in front of you.
- **Later:** you sense where treasure is, and enemies that are off-screen.
- If you lose smell, gas enemies that use pepper bombs to stun you do nothing.
- Having smell is constant visual clutter, so there may be an incentive to remove it.
- People have a slightly translucent blob on them until you lose smell.
- **Smelling that an enemy is bleeding** shows how healthy that enemy is.
- **Smell with agi:** you can smell bombs on enemies, and a button sets them off.
- **Smell with str:** something else shows where enemies are, even if it's just a moving blob. It is more real-time than the hearing sonar wave.
- He also floated, as an early idea: "maybe smell for strength or agility?"

### The leg(s)
Two options:
- You stop walking and start blinking.
- *Or* the slower you walk, the more damage you can take and the harder you hit.

(He said "the movement and legs thing can be changed maybe.")

### Intellect: the companion sprite
- A sprite travels with you, like Ori, and talks to you.
- It gently suggests things and guides the player so they don't take combinations like going blind *and* deaf.
  - If the player does it anyway, the sprite gets sarcastic, or genuinely talks slowly to the player.
- **Or** the sprite is part of the senses, essentially **intellect**. It is part of the protagonist's cognition talking to them. It helps by:
  - guiding,
  - helping in combat by showing things or telling you what to do,
  - being helpful even when you're blind, working through smell or hearing: "that smells like a brute" or "that sounds like a brute" (or any enemy type).
- It works as an aide in the roguelite.
- **As a sense you can trade away, played for comedy:** at the end, for the trade, you see a bottle. The PC is an alcoholic. The sprite says things like "don't drink it, we are sober." If the player drinks it, they lose the sprite, and the dialogue becomes funny, like Fallout's low-INT dialogue.

## Str / Agi

- At the very start, the player can choose **strength, agility, or neither**. This choice sets up the tone of the game and the gameplay.
- Reason for putting it at the start: if the game blinds or disables the player too fast, it could be rough on a lot of people.
- It works like Dead Cells: a build that is faster but weaker, or the reverse.
- Agi/str might always be the first choice. (He put a pin in that.)
- If the player picks str or agi, then even while not blind they see sonar pulses from their own walking and from things breaking. If they pick neither, they don't get the pulses ("but that can come later").

### Feel: Muhammad Ali vs Mike Tyson
- **Agility:**
  - Fast kinetic feedback.
  - A glass cannon, but very fast.
- **Strength:**
  - Slow but thumpy.
  - You see things slower and a bit late. If you anticipate properly, the whacks need to resound.

### Pulses by lens
- **Blind + agi:** more pulses, and the character does more footwork, but the pulses don't go as far.
- **Blind + str:** slower pulses, but they go farther.

### Parry by lens
- **Agi:** a larger parry window, less damage.
- **Str:** very high damage on a parry, but a very short window. A little **"heh"** sound plays if a strength build lands a parry counter.

---

## Buffs and builds

- The big buffs come at the end, from sacrificing a sense. Sacrificing one thing sharpens the others by a larger degree.
- There are also a lot of mini, small buffs with a ton of synergy, so players can make cracked builds, like Risk of Rain or Synthetik. The small ones work like the ones in Dead Cells or Risk of Rain.
- The str/agi drug is the only "good" drug the protagonist gets. The rest are experimental, which is why each one enhances one thing and removes another.

---

## The boss and the debuff economy

- If players reach the boss with no downsides taken, the boss has to be brutal.
- If they take the debuffs, the boss is built around these principles: if they show up as a jack-of-all-trades Swiss knife, they have the tools but not the damage.
- The design forces a choice: take the debuffs, or accept that it's going to be a long, arduous battle.
- The more debuffs a player takes, the more challenge they have to deal with, but the considerably more damage they do to the boss.

## Story

### Tone and frame
- Cyberpunk BioShock.
- The buffs and debuffs are like **ADAM from BioShock**: auto-injection syringes the protagonist finds at the end of levels. He decides whether to jam one in or not.
- You can't have a heroic protagonist doing unmarked pharmaceutical drugs to keep going, so the protagonist has to be like **Harry** (Disco Elysium).
- He is a drunk, found passed out, the last hope.

### The protagonist
- An old, washed-up, drunk operative. **Greenway** from the new 007 game (the grizzled mentor of the new 007), or a washed-up **Shifu**.
- (He briefly floated "flip the script where the protag is young," then settled on old.)
- If the protagonist wins, he's still just an old, washed-up drunk operative.
- He was the **mentor of the villain**, the main vet, and the team lead above the squad lead.
- A mission went bad in the past. The protagonist quit and became a washed-up drunk, and the rest became cutthroat.
- "Future old man for the game."

### The villain
- An evil megacorp villain who found out how to **reset time by one day**. The protagonist found out about it.
- Like **Tai Lung** from Kung Fu Panda, but he won. A mix of Tai Lung and **Handsome Jack**.
- He was the squad leader. The protagonist was his mentor.
- They were best friends (originally floated as "the twist they were buddies or something, later"). The protagonist now hates the villain.
- If the protagonist loses, the villain is still a powerful person in the world.

### The mini-bosses
- One per level, like the **Famous Five**, which gives them more chemistry when talking.
- They were the villain's teammates, part of that same troupe.
- They are young. Initially they're incentivized by greed to go with the villain.
- The villain keeps them, and people generally, on his side by **framing the protagonist as an unhinged lunatic with a dirty bomb**.
- The player becomes friends with the bosses over multiple runs, and their respect for each other deepens.
- **How they relate to the player, as it evolved:**
  1. On the destroy-the-drugs path, the mini-bosses don't fight the protagonist. They join him when he reaches them, then show up at the final boss to take away some of the boss's advantages.
  2. Later: no freeing them and no option of not fighting them. It will always be fighting and killing, because these are hardened operatives. But if he takes the nuke underground to where the formulas and manufacturing are, they side with him.
  3. Later: this is why they join towards the end. The villain's framing of the old man as the villain falls apart when he throws the bomb underground where the formulas are. Because he's an addict and old, it will harm him as well. It also gives them an opening to get rid of the main villain. (Each one gets their own reasons, to be written later.)

### The loop and the stalemate
- The protagonist bum-rushes the main building **Johnny Silverhand-style, but it's Harry doing it**.
- The villain has to reset the day because the protagonist has **a bomb with a dead-man's switch**. When the protagonist dies, the villain is forced to reset anyway. This is the stalemate.
- **Where the bomb comes from, as it evolved:**
  1. The protagonist entered with it.
  2. Later: the protagonist makes it to the building and finds someone close to him dead, with the dead-man's switch. He decides to finish the job.
- The bomb is a **big dirty nuke**, which adds moral weight: the city would die with the villain.
- **Everyone keeps knowledge of the reset:** the villain, the hero, the enemies, and the mini-bosses.

### Starting outside, and levels that adapt
- The first level, or prologue, is **outside, without powers**. It shows the state of the city and its people.
- Because the villain resets the day, the protagonist **always starts outside**.
- **Dynamic levels:** the villains know he's coming. They set up more people or fewer, arrange things according to how the last run went, and so on. It has to feel like the villains are also adapting, not cartoon villains just waiting each time.

### Banter
- A running commentary: witty and tragic two-man banter, with both sides trying to reason with, threaten, and otherwise work on each other. Think **Hades**, including the running commentary on Zag's escape.
- Even some of the enemies talk to you.
- A pseudo-nemesis system, "if we won't get sued."

### The drugs
- Through multiple runs, the player learns the ADAM-style drug is **extracted unethically**.
- The protagonist discovers the drugs are for the **mass market**.
- The villain mentions that a lot of people on the drugs will die if they stop. Not from withdrawal, but from the changes in their bodies.
  - Not everyone dies. For example, an older man taking str drugs who stops and picks something up like he did when he was young will have his bones break. Take str or agi away from someone who has atrophied without it, and it's over for them.
  - People who were working out while taking it are fine.
  - Still, a lot of people will die. Not the mini-bosses or the protagonist, but anyone in their families taking the meds.
- A few friendly NPCs are implied to die if the drugs stop. This is another moral choice.

### How the villain can actually die
- The protagonist has to learn that the villain resets the day himself.
- He needs to find a way to disable the villain's manual trigger, so that only the **automatic trigger at the end of the day** still works.
- The bomb is a crude solution: it blows everything up, and the time machine goes with it. But he can also destroy the machine without the bomb.

### Endings and moral choices
- The central framing: **revenge vs. does the player care about society in general.**
- **Bomb to the formulas:** once the protagonist discovers the drugs are for the mass market, he can choose to move the bomb there and detonate it where the formulas are. It won't kill the villain. If he fails that loop, **the game deletes the save file**, like Outer Wilds: you discover the thing that's resetting, and if you mess up, there are no more resets.
  - On this path he destroys the drugs, and if he fails, the villain gets everything.
- **Don't destroy the syringes, kill the villain:** the company is implied to keep selling drugs, which is bad for society, but you prevented them from getting time travel.
- **Take the drugs out and go for it:** you get the best ending, but you have to rely on the skills you built up, in a non-forgiving run against a fully souped-up boss.
- **True ending:** locked behind the protagonist never engaging with the system. He beats the villain with knowledge from multiple runs, finding other things that weaken the villain: things he can physically do before reaching the boss. This takes multiple runs.
- Even the **secret best ending** has to be earned Dark Souls-style, like **giving the eyes to the Fire Keeper**.

## Later lore direction: living forever

He introduced this as a direction change "to keep it lighter," then followed it with "grim is good." Both are recorded below in order.

- The tale is a consequence of things going too far. They found a way to **live forever**, but the human psyche isn't meant for it.
- The **DMN (default mode network)** exists to keep you alive: to keep you gathering things, keep you feeling pleasure, keep you dominating for safety. Everyone is railing against dying, because the DMN keeps pushing them forward.
- **Memory:** the longer someone lives, the more new memories they have to contain. Eventually they essentially forget millennia and keep only their original memories. That means they need people and machines to remember the middle period. If a human took in that much knowledge, the brain's software couldn't run it. You would have to make new software, and if you did, it would no longer be you.
- The most affluent essentially stay at their peak, reliving their glory times and memories.
- **Bosses as a result:**
  - **The Brute** kept being the brute. He is like a heavy armored core, almost fused with machinery and weapons.
  - **Lust** is someone who gave in and pursued only worldly, pleasurable pursuits.
  - **The Librarian** is maybe a hidden boss.
- The poor are left to figure it out. Most are sentient but have memories spanning only 200–300 years. The rich try to alleviate this.
- **Twist:** the protagonist is the person responsible for starting all of this. He can somehow end the living-forever experiment ("have to come up with something here"). Maybe the villains control the thing that lets everyone live forever, such as a machine that encodes thought into code.
- **Grim version:** they were **mercenaries who stole the tech and killed its creators eons ago**. They don't want it getting out, so they keep it guarded.
- The dark part: **friends and family of the protagonist are also living forever.**
- He sees themes of **Ptolemy, Alexander, and the other generals**: essentially mercenaries who established themselves as gods in Egypt, Rome, and elsewhere, a kind of immortality.

## The opening scene (as he described it)

- One enemy throws a spear. His words on the throw: "even that I don't want like arms moving." It is done the way anime takes pauses:
  1. The enemy cocks his hand back, then a **micropause**.
  2. He throws, then a **micropause**, to show it's going fast.
  3. It flies.
  4. When it reaches the protagonist, he **cocks his head to the side**, dodging it, and **catches it with his hand where his head was**, then a **micropause**.
- The animation is smooth, with micropauses for scenes like that.
- Gameplay works the same way: Dead Cells-fast and smooth, a nanopause on every hit, and buttery dopamine-hit sound effects.
